What thirty years of deals actually bought

The world's biggest seed companies spent three decades buying each other, then buying software companies, then buying biology companies. This dashboard sets out, in plain terms, what they paid, what came back, and which parts of the story the numbers actually support.

01

The story in four stages

A seed company sells farmers the seed they plant. That business has always been about genetics — breeding plants that yield more, resist disease, or tolerate the weedkillers a farmer uses. Over thirty years the industry consolidated from dozens of companies into essentially four, and what it was buying changed completely at each stage.

STAGE ONE
1996 — 2002

Buying the plants

Companies with new gene technology needed crops to put the genes into. So they bought seed companies for their libraries of plant varieties, built up over decades. DuPont paid $9.4bn for Pioneer; Monsanto bought DeKalb, Asgrow and others.

STAGE TWO
2003 — 2012

Buying traits and vegetables

Next came specific engineered characteristics — insect resistance, herbicide tolerance — plus a push into vegetable seed. Monsanto paid $1.4bn for Seminis in 2005. In 2012 it bought a planting-equipment company, its first purchase of hardware and data.

STAGE THREE
2013 — 2019

Buying software, then each other

Monsanto paid $930m for a weather-and-farm-data company in 2013. Within four years every rival had bought something similar. Then the industry merged into four groups in deals worth over $200bn combined.

STAGE FOUR
2020 — 2026

Buying biology, and breaking up

Attention moved to biological products and to artificial intelligence used in laboratories. Meanwhile Corteva announced it will split itself in two by the end of 2026 — the first reversal of the whole merger era.

02

What the buyers could — and couldn't — point to

First, one piece of accounting, explained

When a company buys another company, it has to tell its auditors exactly what it bought. Valuers go through the target and put a price on everything they can identify: factories, patents, brands, customer contracts, technology.

Whatever's left over — the gap between the price paid and the sum of identifiable things — is recorded as goodwill. Goodwill is not a thing you can sell. It is the accounting record of how much a buyer paid for control and expectation rather than for assets.

So the share of a purchase price recorded as goodwill tells you how much the buyer's own valuers could actually put their finger on. A low share means they bought identifiable things. A high share means they mostly bought hope.

How each purchase was accounted for

The dark portion is identifiable assets like technology, brands and customer relationships. Where a bar goes past the dashed line, the buyer also took on debts along with the business.

The top bar is the point. When Monsanto paid $932m for The Climate Corporation in 2013 — the deal that started the whole farm-software race — its valuers could identify only $142m of assets. That was $138m of technology and $4m of customer relationships, which they wrote off within a year. No database, no weather archive, no collection of farm data appears anywhere on the list. The remaining 84% was recorded as goodwill.

Compare the bottom bar. When BASF bought seed and software businesses that regulators forced Bayer to sell in 2018, only 17% was goodwill — because a forced sale has no bidding war and no premium. It is the cheapest quality-per-euro deal in the industry's history, and BASF got it by not competing.

03

Seeds got much more profitable. Chemicals didn't.

The measure being used

Profit margin here means: of every $100 of sales, how many dollars are left as operating profit before interest, tax, and the accounting charge for equipment wearing out. It's the standard way to compare how much money a business keeps from what it sells.

Corteva is the only one of the big four that publishes separate profit figures for its seed business and its crop-chemicals business. That makes it the only clean view anyone has of which half is actually working.

The two businesses started in the same place in 2020 and have gone in different directions. The seed business now keeps $26.60 of every $100 it sells; crop chemicals keeps $18.00. Chemical sales actually peaked in 2022 and are 11.5% lower today. Every dollar of Corteva's growth since then has come from seed.

What caused the improvement is not what most people assume. Corteva's own explanation to investors lists price increases, cost cuts, and licensing income — money it now collects from rivals for using its plant technology. Software and data are not mentioned. Not once, in any year.

04

Vegetables look like the premium business. They aren't.

There is a long-standing belief in the industry that vegetable seed — tomatoes, spinach, broccoli — is a better business than ordinary field crops like corn and soybeans. Vegetable seed sells for 50 to 80 times more per seed. The gross profit margins are genuinely higher.

Two independent pieces of evidence say the belief is wrong once you look past gross profit.

KWS: profit or loss on every €100 of sales, by crop, 2024/25
KWS is a German seed company and the only listed business in the world that reports its vegetable operation separately from its field crops. Bars to the right of the line are profits; bars to the left are losses.

KWS bought a profitable Dutch spinach seed company in 2019 with roughly €75m of sales, then invested heavily to build a full vegetable business around it. Six years on, the whole vegetable division has €72.1m of sales and loses €45.8m a year. It is 4.3% of the company's sales and consumes 15.5% of its investment spending.

Why it's so hard: vegetable breeding doesn't share anything between crops. Work on tomatoes teaches you nothing about onions or spinach. In corn, one engineered characteristic can be sold across an entire product range. In vegetables you pay the full cost, separately, thirty times over.

Second piece of accounting, explained

Every year, companies must check whether the businesses on their books are still worth what the books say. They forecast future cash and discount it back to today's money. If the forecast comes out lower than the recorded value, they must write the value down — an impairment. If an earlier write-down now looks too harsh, they can write some of it back up — a reversal.

These judgements are reviewed by auditors. They are the closest thing to an honest internal opinion that a company ever publishes about its own divisions.

Bayer's own valuation review, 2025
In 2025 Bayer re-examined every part of its crop business alongside a full strategy revision. Green bars are write-ups; the red bar is a write-down. Figures in millions of euros.

In one review, Bayer's own models — checked by its auditors — raised the value of ordinary field-crop seed by €1,036m and cut the value of vegetable seed by €196m. Bayer's vegetable sales then fell 12% in the first quarter of 2026.

Twenty-one years after Monsanto paid $1.4bn for the vegetable company Seminis, that business is €788m of sales — 3.6% of Bayer's crop division. On the multiples the market pays for comparable seed companies, it's now worth roughly €1.0–1.2bn, against about $3.5bn in today's money for what was paid.

05

Did the software and data money achieve anything?

The financial test is easy to state and easy to fail: not one seed company anywhere reports software or data revenue — not as a business line, not as a segment, not even as a figure mentioned in passing. Bayer's platform is used on 250 million acres across 23 countries and contributes no disclosed revenue.

But revenue isn't the only thing an investment can deliver. So it's worth testing six things it was supposed to improve, one at a time.

Best evidence

Running the factories better

Real improvement — cause unproven

Corteva built a "digital twin" — a working computer model of its whole seed operation, from design through production to delivery — to plan better and waste less.

Its biggest single source of profit growth in 2025 was cost reduction, ahead of both price increases and higher volumes. But the company doesn't say how much of that came from the software rather than from closing sites and cutting headcount.

Corteva used to pay Bayer to use its plant technology. It now collects more than it pays. A settlement with Bayer brought that crossover forward by two years.

Breeding new varieties faster

A 2030 target, not yet visible

Bayer says AI-guided breeding will cut the time to develop a new variety from six years to four months, and more than double the rate of improvement by 2030. If true it would be the biggest change in the history of plant breeding.

But it's a target. No company publishes how long its breeding actually takes today. And US corn yields — the ultimate scoreboard — are still improving at the same steady rate they have for decades, with some evidence of slowing.

Predicting demand

The opposite of what was intended

If a model of your seed business works, you should plant the right amount, hold less unsold stock, and get paid faster.

In 2025 Corteva's unpaid customer bills grew four times faster than its sales. Money tied up in unpaid bills and unsold stock rose from 65.7% to 69.2% of annual sales. Some of this may be deliberate credit to customers in South America — but it is the opposite of what the tool is meant to produce.

Corteva rebuilt its failed farmer software into an internal tool for its own sales representatives, and gave them a ChatGPT-style assistant trained on its private agricultural research.

This is a sensible use of what it bought. But no company publishes sales conversion rates, customer retention, or rep productivity, so there is no way to check whether it works.

06

The fairest comparison: what happened to everyone else

Judged on their own, the seed companies' software purchases look poor. But the right question isn't "did they make money?" — it's "compared to what?" Several companies tried to build the same thing as independent businesses, funded by investors betting on exactly this idea.

Value lost by independent farm-technology companies
Fall from peak valuation to final outcome.

Farmers Edge floated in 2021 at C$17.00 a share and was taken private three years later at C$0.35. Indigo Ag fell from a $3.5bn valuation to $200m. Benson Hill filed for bankruptcy in March 2025. Across the sector, more than 21 farm-technology businesses went bankrupt or were liquidated in 2025 alone.

Seen this way, the picture changes. Monsanto bought The Climate Corporation in October 2013 — before the funding boom — and still owns it. It didn't make money on the deal. But it avoided the losses the market handed to everyone who backed the same idea as an investment rather than as insurance.

07

The number that explains the whole thirty years

One more measure, explained

Return on invested capital answers a simple question: for every $100 tied up in this business, how much profit does it produce in a year? If that number is lower than what the money costs to borrow and raise — usually 8–9% for a company like this — then the business is running to stand still.

Corteva's return on the money invested in it
The dashed line marks roughly what Corteva's money costs. The second bar shows the same operations with the accumulated cost of past acquisitions stripped out.

Corteva's actual seed and chemicals operations are outstanding — they earn roughly 56 cents a year for every dollar of real, physical capital in them. But $18.8bn of Corteva's balance sheet isn't factories or inventory. It is the recorded cost of the mergers that created the company.

Carrying that cost drags the return down to about 9% — roughly the same as what the money costs. In plain terms: the businesses are excellent. The prices paid for them were not. That single sentence is the honest summary of thirty years of consolidation.

09

What to watch next

  • How Corteva splits its technology when it breaks in two at the end of 2026. Its computer model of the seed business, its AI research assistant and its data systems were built across both halves of the company. Nobody has said which side gets them. That is a real, unpriced risk for both new companies.
  • Whether Bayer sells its vegetable seed business. Syngenta has already done the equivalent — in May 2026 it handed its flower seed business to a specialist partner while keeping a financial stake. It is the sensible template, and Bayer's vegetable business fits the same description.
  • Whether anyone ever publishes how long their breeding actually takes. If a company starts disclosing it, that will be because the number finally improved.
  • Whether Bayer restores its research spending. It cut crop research by 22.9% in 2025, to €2.0bn, at exactly the moment rivals are accelerating. That is not sustainable for long.
  • Whether Corteva's unpaid customer bills come back down without cash flow falling. That would show the record 2025 cash generation was real earnings quality rather than timing.
