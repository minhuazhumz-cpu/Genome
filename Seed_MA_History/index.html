Seed, data, and goodwill

Thirty years of consolidation in the agricultural seed industry, read through the one document that forces a buyer to say what it thinks it bought: the purchase price allocation.

01

The short version

The industry consolidated in four waves, and only the third was about data. What the majors bought changed each time: germplasm, then traits, then software, and now biology and discovery AI.

Digitalization was defensive from the beginning. Monsanto's $930M purchase of The Climate Corporation in October 2013 was the trigger event, and within four years every peer had bought or built a farm management platform. None of them were building a software business. They were preventing a neutral data layer from forming between the seed bag and the field — because whoever owns the recommendation owns the sale.

Every attempt to charge farmers for that software failed. Corteva sold Granular Business to Traction Ag and shut down Granular Agronomy on 31 August 2022, exiting SaaS four years after paying $300M for it. The platforms that survived — FieldView, Cropwise, xarvio — are free, bundled, and exist to steer product choice and harvest agronomic data back into breeding.

WAVE I
1996 — 2002

Germplasm

Buy the seed companies; rent the genomics. Monsanto licensed Millennium's platform for $218M; Syngenta paid Myriad to sequence rice. Both units were closed within a few years.

WAVE II
2003 — 2012

Traits & vegetables

Seminis, Delta & Pine Land, De Ruiter. Then the hinge: Precision Planting in 2012 gave a seed company planter-level telemetry for the first time.

WAVE III
2013 — 2019

Software, then each other

The digital land grab, followed immediately by Big Six → Big Four. Regulators treated farm data as a market, not a feature.

WAVE IV
2020 — 2026

Biology & discovery AI

Biologicals bought at scale, AI moved upstream into molecule discovery, and Corteva announced the first structural reversal of the merger era.

02

What the buyers actually booked

Press releases report a headline number. Purchase price allocation reports what a buyer's own valuers could identify, name, and assign a useful life to. Everything they could not identify becomes goodwill. Across nine deals pulled from primary filings, the goodwill share ranges from 17% to 84% — and the spread maps precisely onto what was being bought.

The number that reframes the story

Of the $932M fair value Monsanto assigned to The Climate Corporation, $783M was goodwill. The identifiable intangibles came to $142M, and they were exactly two things: acquired intellectual property of $138M on a ten-year life, and customer relationships of $4M on a one-year life.

There is no data asset on that list. No database, no weather archive, no agronomic model library. The most celebrated data acquisition in the history of agriculture produced $138M of amortizable technology and a customer relationship written off inside twelve months. The rest was, in Monsanto's own words, the premium paid "for the right to control the acquired business and technology" — and it was not deductible for tax.

Farm data was never a separable, capitalizable asset. It was an option on future product placement, and options get booked as goodwill. The accounting reading of the digital land grab

Three things the filings say that the press never did

Bayer is the only company that has ever put a farm data platform on a balance sheet. In the Monsanto allocation, Bayer separately identified digital platforms as an intangible asset class with a 15-year useful life, alongside seeds and traits (9–30 years), herbicides (20 years), and brands (10–30 years). IFRS 3 forced a disclosure that voluntary reporting has never produced — though Bayer disclosed the class and the life, not the amount.

Granular effectively never existed in the financial record. A full-text search of EDGAR returns it only inside Exhibit 21 — the subsidiaries list. A $300M acquisition received no purchase price allocation, no segment disclosure, no impairment note, and no gain or loss on divestiture when it was sold to Traction Ag. Monsanto's entire digital bolt-on programme — 640 Labs, Solum, HydroBio, VitalFields — produced no business-combination disclosure at all. The land grab that reshaped the industry's competitive narrative was, in accounting terms, a rounding error attached to one $932M anchor deal.

Several "undisclosed" prices were disclosed all along. The FY2014 Monsanto 10-K carries fair values for deals the trade press reported without numbers: Beeologics at $113M, Agradis at $85M, Rosetta Green at $35M, Dieckmann at $30M, GrassRoots Biotechnology at $15M. Beeologics alone is far larger than the tuck-in it was described as, and it makes Monsanto's 2011–13 biologicals programme a roughly $280M bet placed a decade before Corteva paid $1.59B for Stoller and Symborg.

05

Five theses

  1. Traits pulled the first consolidation; data pulled the second.Wave I was about having crops to put genes into. Wave III was about having fields to put sensors on. Both were about controlling a distribution channel for R&D output.
  2. Vertical data integration was attempted, then dismantled.Monsanto's 2012–16 stack — planter hardware, telematics, agronomic modelling, seed — was the most complete vertical integration ever assembled in agriculture. Antitrust took the hardware layer; economics took the subscription layer. What survived is a free data-collection front end attached to a seed business.
  3. The equipment OEMs won the machine layer.Deere (Blue River, Bear Flag) and AGCO (Precision Planting, PTx Trimble) own the actuator and the in-cab interface. Seed companies became guests on OEM platforms — which is why FieldView's two-way link to John Deere Operations Center matters more than any feature it ships.
  4. AI moved upstream because that is where willingness-to-pay is.A farmer will not pay $3 an acre for an app. A crop-protection business will fund anything that compresses a decade-long active-ingredient discovery cycle. Hence Insilico, Enko, Profluent, Ginkgo.
  5. The scale logic is now contested.Corteva's 2025 split says seed and chemistry no longer need each other — a direct repudiation of the 2015–18 thesis. If it closes cleanly and both entities re-rate, expect the same pressure on Bayer.
06

Open questions

  • How Corteva divides its AI and data estate. CARL, the seed-production digital twin, and the Granular models were built across seed and chemistry. The separation allocates them to one side, and nothing has been disclosed. It is a direct valuation input for both entities and the sharpest unanswered question in the sector.
  • Bayer's R&D trajectory. Crop Science R&D fell 22.9% to €2.01B in 2025. That is not sustainable alongside an AI-discovery race. Either it reverses, or Bayer becomes a licensor.
  • Syngenta's funding path. After the withdrawn $9B Shanghai listing, is the open Cropwise platform genuine ecosystem strategy or a capital-light substitute for building?
  • Whether ONE SMART SPRAY scales across OEMs. If BASF's agronomic engine ends up inside Deere, AGCO, and CNH machines, BASF holds the highest-margin digital position in the industry without owning a platform.
  • Licensing between majors. The logical endgame is a small number of agronomic foundation models trained on proprietary trial data. Watch for model or data licensing deals between the four — historically unthinkable, now economically rational.
